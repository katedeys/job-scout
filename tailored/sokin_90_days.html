Payment Success at Sokin — my first 90 days
Product Manager, Payment Success · Belgrade · katedeys@gmail.com · linkedin.com/in/kate-deys

This plan is written from the outside, based on the job description and how cross-border B2B payments usually break. I would treat every point as a hypothesis, test it against Sokin's data in the first two weeks, and rewrite the plan with the team. The goal stays the same: more payments go straight through, and clients always know where their money is.

How I would measure payment success

MetricWhy it matters
Straight-through processing rateShare of payments that complete with no manual repair or client follow-up. The headline number for the role.

Repairs and manual touches per 1,000 paymentsThe operations cost of every gap in the rules.

Time to final status, by corridorSpeed clients feel; the base for honest ETAs.

Days 1–30 · Learn and set the baseline

Outcome: one shared picture of where payments fail and what each failure costs.
  • Map the payment lifecycle end to end: creation and rules, validation, routing, submission to partners, confirmation, tracking, returns.
  • Build a failure taxonomy from transaction-level data: rejections by rule, partner, corridor, currency and reason code. Size each bucket in payments, money and ops hours.
  • Sit with Operations, Support and Compliance on real repairs and tickets; learn which checks exist for regulatory reasons and must stay.
  • Agree baseline metrics and one dashboard the tribe trusts; ship 2–3 quick wins (clearer validation messages, missing pre-checks on beneficiary data).

Days 31–60 · Fix the biggest leaks

Outcome: the top three failure buckets measurably smaller.
  • Move checks to payment creation: corridor-specific beneficiary and purpose-code rules, so bad payments are fixed by the client before submission instead of repaired by Ops after.
  • One internal payment state model with clear reason codes, mapped from every partner's responses. At inDrive this single model is what made outcomes consistent for finance and support.
  • Client-facing tracking built on that model: plain-language status, the reason when something is held, and the next step.
  • Partner health monitoring and alerts (backlogs, timeouts, reject spikes), so degradation is caught before clients notice.

Days 61–90 · Make it scale and prepare the ML bets

Outcome: a platform regional teams can build on, and a ranked roadmap for H1.
  • Routing and fallback rules per corridor by success, speed and cost, with a clear owner and change process.
  • Pick the first ML use case by data readiness and value. Likely candidates from the JD: failure prediction before submission, delivery-time prediction for ETAs, routing decisions. Start with one pilot and a clear success metric.
  • A monthly payment success review with Engineering, Ops and Compliance, plus a roadmap that balances fixes, platform work and expansion needs.

What I bring to this

  • Routing, failover and provider health: payment orchestration across 40 countries at inDrive, cost-based routing (~$100K/month saved), failover on soft/hard declines, 96.4% provider SLA.
  • Correct, auditable money movement: disbursements with idempotency, retries and reconciliation; PSP, EMI and bank integration contracts with error mapping (SkyRo).
  • Compliance as a partner: led PCI DSS Service Provider certification with Security and Compliance.

Questions I would bring to the first week: which corridors carry most volume and most repairs; where STP is lost today (creation, partner rejects or returns); how repairs are handled now; which partner signals we already capture. Most of my hands-on experience is with card and PSP flows; cross-border rails and local schemes are where I would invest time first.
